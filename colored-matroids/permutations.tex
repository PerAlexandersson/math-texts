\section[permutationMatroids]{Permutation matroids}

\begin{definition}[Permutation matroid]
A \defin{permutation matroid} is a colored matroid in which every color
has quota one. Its words form a nonempty family $\mathcal F\subseteq S_n$.
\end{definition}

We view a permutation $u$ as a map from positions to colors, with entry
$u_e=u(e)$, and compose maps from right to left. Write $X(u)=\chi(u)$ for
its \href{polytope.htm#polytope}{assignment matrix}. The convex hull of all
permutation matrices is the \defin{Birkhoff polytope} $B_n$.

The singleton quotas make every prescribed exchange unique. This gives
both a closure operation and a reconstruction from the neighbors of one
word. When one vertex is simple, the resulting polytopes are exactly
\href{stable-sets.htm#simpleVertexClassification}{stable-set polytopes of
graphs with no even hole}, up to real affine isomorphism.

\section[relativeCycles]{Closure under relative cycles}

\begin{theorem}[Relative-cycle closure]
A nonempty family $\mathcal F\subseteq S_n$ is a permutation matroid
if and only if, for every $u,v\in\mathcal F$ with disjoint nontrivial
cycle decomposition
\[
 u^{-1}v=c_1\dotsm c_r,
\]
we have
\[
 u\prod_{j\in J}c_j\in\mathcal F
 \qquad\text{for every }J\subseteq[r].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The disagreement cycle through a position in $\operatorname{supp}(c_j)$
is unique: its color arrows are the image of $c_j$ under $u$.
Symmetric cycle exchange therefore forces the switch of this cycle.
Starting at $u$, switch the cycles indexed by $J$ one at a time,
comparing the current word with $v$. This gives the displayed word.
Conversely, the choices $J=\{j\}$ and $J=[r]\setminus\{j\}$ give
both offspring for a prescribed disagreement in $c_j$.
\end{proof}

For example, containing $\mathtt{1234}$ and $\mathtt{2143}$ forces
$\mathtt{2134}$ and $\mathtt{1243}$. Every nonempty subset of $S_3$
is a permutation matroid, since two permutations on three positions
differ on at most one nontrivial cycle.

\begin{corollary}[Intersections and completion]
Every nonempty intersection of permutation matroids on the same positions
and colors is a permutation matroid. Every nonempty seed family has a
unique smallest permutation matroid containing it.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each family in the intersection contains the forced recombinations of
every pair in the intersection. For a seed family, intersect all
permutation matroids containing it; $S_n$ is one such family.
\end{proof}

One-sided cycle exchange also suffices: apply it to both ordered pairs
$(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$. Uniqueness gives the two offspring of the same cycle.

\subsection[cycleCrossover]{Cycle crossover}

In evolutionary algorithms, \defin{cycle crossover} recombines two parent
permutations by choosing which parent supplies each relative cycle.
The operation goes back to I.~M.~Oliver, D.~J.~Smith and J.~R.~C.~Holland
(1987); see the description and references in
\href{https://eden.dei.uc.pt/~moraglio/ppsn-workshop.pdf}{Moraglio and
coauthors}. Allowing every choice of parental cycles, relative-cycle
closure says exactly that a nonempty family is a permutation matroid
if and only if every cycle-crossover offspring remains feasible.

\subsection[hammingConvexity]{Convexity in the permutation Hamming space}

Let $d_H(u,v)=|\{e:u_e\ne v_e\}|$ be Hamming distance. The
\defin{metric interval} between $u,v\in S_n$ is
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \defin{[u,v]_H}\coloneqq\{w\in S_n:\\
 d_H(u,w)+d_H(w,v)=d_H(u,v)\}.
 \end{gathered}
\]
A family $\mathcal F\subseteq S_n$ is \defin{metrically convex} if
$[u,v]_H\subseteq\mathcal F$ for all $u,v\in\mathcal F$.

\begin{proposition}[Metric convexity]
If $u^{-1}v=c_1\dotsm c_r$ is the disjoint nontrivial cycle
decomposition, then
\[
 [u,v]_H=
 \left\{u\prod_{j\in J}c_j:J\subseteq[r]\right\}.
\]
Consequently, the nonempty metrically convex subsets of $S_n$ are
exactly the permutation matroids.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equality in the Hamming triangle inequality holds precisely when
$w_e\in\{u_e,v_e\}$ at every position. Since $w$ is a permutation,
choosing the entry from $v$ at one disagreement position forces the
same choice around its entire relative cycle. Conversely, choosing
whole relative cycles gives a permutation satisfying the equality.
The characterization now follows from relative-cycle closure.
\end{proof}

Thus the smallest permutation matroid containing a seed family is both
its cycle-crossover closure and its metric convex hull in $S_n$.
For the general connection between metric convexity and evolutionary
search, see A.~Moraglio,
\href{https://eden.dei.uc.pt/~moraglio/foga24e-moraglio.pdf}{Abstract
Convex Evolutionary Search} (2011).

\subsection[permutationLiterature]{Related structures and literature}

For subgroup families, our condition is precisely the
\href{#permutationGroups}{cycle-closed condition} studied by
P.~J.~Cameron in 1996. His classification gives the group theorem below.

\begin{remark}[Coxeter matroids]
Coxeter matroids use root directions in a permutohedron; see
\href{https://iris.univpm.it/bitstream/11566/294024/9/CaselliDadderioMariettiREVISED.pdf}{Caselli,
D'Adderio and Marietti}. The two classes differ. The family
$\{\mathtt{123},\mathtt{231}\}$ is a permutation matroid, but its
one-line vectors span a segment with direction $(1,1,-2)$, which is
not parallel to a type-$A$ root $e_i-e_j$.
\end{remark}

\section[birkhoffEdges]{The polyhedral characterization}

\begin{lemma}[Cube edges]
A nonempty polytope whose vertices are cube vertices and whose edges are
cube edges is a face of the cube.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let a coordinate vary on the polytope. At each vertex, some other vertex
has the opposite value of this coordinate. The direction to that vertex
is a nonnegative combination of incident edge directions. Since these
are coordinate directions, one incident edge must flip the chosen
coordinate. Thus every varying coordinate can be flipped at every
vertex. All combinations of these flips occur, giving a cube face.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Birkhoff edges]
A nonempty family $\mathcal F\subseteq S_n$ is a permutation matroid
if and only if every edge of $P(\mathcal F)$ is an edge of $B_n$.
Its graph is then the induced Birkhoff graph on $\mathcal F$: two words
are adjacent exactly when their relative permutation has one
nontrivial cycle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a permutation matroid, the
\href{polytope.htm#polytopeEdges}{cycle-edge theorem} identifies its
edges with single relative cycles, which are edges of $B_n$.

Conversely, take $u,v\in\mathcal F$. Restrict each position to its
two parent entries. The resulting face of $B_n$ is a cube, with one
coordinate for each nontrivial cycle of $u^{-1}v$. Its vertices are
the relative-cycle recombinations. Its intersection with $P(\mathcal F)$
is a face of $P(\mathcal F)$, has only cube edges, and contains the
opposite corners $X(u),X(v)$. By the lemma it is the whole cube.
Relative-cycle closure proves symmetric cycle exchange.

Finally, an edge of $B_n$ with both endpoints in $P(\mathcal F)$ remains
an edge of this subpolytope, by restricting an exposing score.
\end{proof}

\section[rootCycles]{Reconstruction from one vertex}

Let $\mathcal F$ be a permutation matroid, and relabel colors so that
a chosen word is the identity. Let $\mathcal C$
be the feasible permutations having exactly one nontrivial cycle.
We identify each such permutation with that cycle. These are precisely
the neighbors of the identity.

Let $H$ be the graph on $\mathcal C$ in which two cycles are adjacent
when their supports intersect. A \defin{stable set} of a graph is a set
of pairwise nonadjacent vertices. Its \defin{stable-set polytope} is
\[
 \operatorname{STAB}(H)=
 \operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf1_S:S\text{ stable}\}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Reconstruction]
The feasible root cycles determine the family:
\[
 \mathcal F=
 \left\{\prod_{\gamma\in S}\gamma:
 S\text{ is a stable set of }H\right\}.
\]
These products give a bijection between stable sets and feasible words.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Compare a feasible word with the identity. Relative-cycle closure puts
each of its individual cycles in $\mathcal C$, and these have disjoint
supports. Conversely, suppose a product of disjoint feasible cycles has
already been constructed. Compare it with a further feasible cycle on
a disjoint support and switch that cycle. The enlarged product is
feasible. Uniqueness is ordinary disjoint-cycle decomposition.
\end{proof}

Write
\[
 \delta_\gamma=X(\gamma)-I,
 \qquad D=[\,\delta_\gamma\,]_{\gamma\in\mathcal C},
\]
regarding matrices as vectors with $n^2$ entries.
Disjoint cycles change disjoint rows, so reconstruction gives
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 P(\mathcal F)=I+D\operatorname{STAB}(H),\\
 \dim P(\mathcal F)=\operatorname{rank}D.
 \end{gathered}
\]
The map is bijective on vertices. It is an affine isomorphism precisely
when the columns of $D$ are independent.

\begin{example}[An affine image]
For $\mathcal F=S_3$, the five nonidentity permutations are cycles
with pairwise intersecting supports. Thus $H=K_5$, and its stable-set
polytope is a five-dimensional simplex. Its image is the four-dimensional
$B_3$. The directions satisfy
\[
 \delta_{(123)}+\delta_{(132)}
 =\delta_{(12)}+\delta_{(13)}+\delta_{(23)}.
\]
\end{example}

A vertex of a $d$-dimensional polytope is \defin{simple} if it has
exactly $d$ incident edges. The identity has $|\mathcal C|$ incident
edges, so it is simple exactly when the columns of $D$ are independent.
The \href{stable-sets.htm}{stable-set chapter} classifies this case
and describes its assignment-coordinate facets.

\section[permutationEnumeration]{Small families}

We count nonempty families. Isomorphisms independently relabel positions
and colors, sending $\mathcal F$ to $\alpha\mathcal F\beta$ with
$\alpha,\beta\in S_n$.

Exact enumeration gives the following counts.
\[
\begin{array}{r|r|r}
 n&\text{Labeled}&\text{Classes}\\\hline
 1&1&1\\
 2&3&2\\
 3&63&9\\
 4&29\,411&107\\
 5&130\,459\,889&10\,743
\end{array}
\]
For four positions, only relative cycle type $(2,2)$ forces new words;
for five positions, the types are $(2,2)$ and $(3,2)$.
There are respectively $18$ and $1050$ exchange squares, and the families
are exactly the sets that complete each square when they contain a
diagonal pair. Enumerating these closed sets gives the labeled counts;
Burnside's lemma for $S_n\times S_n$ gives the class counts.

For every $n\geq2$, two-word families have $n-1$ isomorphism classes,
indexed by the length of their unique nontrivial relative cycle.
Their labeled count is
\[
 \frac{n!}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^n\binom n\ell(\ell-1)!.
\]
Normalize one word to the identity; conjugacy then leaves only the
cycle length. For the labeled count, choose the first word and its
relative cycle, and divide by two.

\section[threeParentClosure]{Two parents and three parents}

For $s\geq2$, call a nonempty permutation family
\defin{closed under $s$-parent recombination} if every equality
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^sX(u_i)=\sum_{i=1}^sX(v_i)
\]
with $u_1,\dotsc,u_s\in\mathcal F$ forces
$v_1,\dotsc,v_s\in\mathcal F$. Repetitions are allowed,
and all $v_i$ are permutations.

\begin{theorem}[Recombination closure]
Two-parent closure is equivalent to being a permutation matroid.
Three-parent closure is equivalent to being the complete vertex family
of a nonempty face of $B_n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A decomposition of $X(u)+X(v)$ chooses independently on each alternating
cycle of its bipartite support. Thus two-parent closure is relative-cycle
closure.

Three-parent closure implies two-parent closure by adjoining a repeated
feasible word to a two-parent equality. The cases $n\leq2$ are immediate;
assume $n\geq3$. Put
\[
 M=\sum_{u\in\mathcal F}X(u),\qquad N=|\mathcal F|.
\]
If a permutation $v$ uses only cells occurring in $\mathcal F$, then
$M-X(v)$ is a nonnegative integer matrix with all row and column sums
$N-1$. Repeatedly choosing a supported perfect matching decomposes it
into $N-1$ permutation matrices.

By T.~Yamaguchi, M.~Ogawa and A.~Takemura,
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.1237}{Theorem 2.1},
decompositions of an integer matrix into permutation matrices are
connected by moves replacing at most three summands. Starting with the
decomposition defining $M$, closure keeps every summand feasible along
a path to the decomposition containing $v$. Hence $v\in\mathcal F$.
The family therefore contains every perfect matching on its occurring
cell support, which is the vertex family of a Birkhoff face.
Conversely, recombination preserves the zero cells defining such a face.
\end{proof}

For instance, $\{\mathtt{123},\mathtt{231},\mathtt{312}\}$ is a
permutation matroid, but its three matrices sum to the same matrix as
those of $\mathtt{213},\mathtt{132},\mathtt{321}$.
It is not three-parent closed.

\section[permutationGroups]{Subgroups and cosets}

\begin{theorem}[Group families]
A subgroup $G\leq S_n$ is a permutation matroid exactly when, on
disjoint supports, it is an independent direct product of natural
symmetric groups and regular cyclic groups of prime order.
The same classification applies after independent position and color
relabelings.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Relative-cycle closure applied to $\operatorname{id},g$ says that each
individual cycle of every $g\in G$ belongs to $G$. Conversely, this
condition applied to $u^{-1}v\in G$ supplies all recombinations.
These are the cycle-closed groups classified by P.~J.~Cameron,
\href{https://emis.muni.cz/journals/JACO/Volume5_4/b38540n353g51u18.fulltext.pdf}{Theorem 1(a)}.
\end{proof}

A symmetric block gives a Birkhoff polytope; a regular cyclic block
of prime order $p$ gives a $(p-1)$-simplex, since its shift matrices
have disjoint supports. Thus the group polytopes are products of
Birkhoff polytopes and simplices. On a cyclic block indexed by
$\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, their equations are
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 x_{i,i+t}=x_{0,t},\qquad x_{0,t}\geq0,\\
 \sum_t x_{0,t}=1.
 \end{gathered}
\]
Together with support zeros and the doubly stochastic descriptions of
symmetric blocks, this gives complete ternary descriptions.

These descriptions can also be made binary. For a cyclic block, replace
the equalities and nonnegativity by
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \sum_t x_{0,t}=1,\qquad \sum_{s\ne t}x_{0,s}\leq1,\\
 x_{i,i+t}+\sum_{s\ne t}x_{0,s}=1\qquad(i\ne0).
 \end{gathered}
\]
For symmetric blocks, keep the row and column sums and write
nonnegativity as $\sum_{d\ne c}x_{e,d}\leq1$.
